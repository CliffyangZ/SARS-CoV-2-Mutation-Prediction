\title{QuSpike-PQK: Projected Quantum Kernel\\
Experts for SARS-CoV-2 Spike Lineage\\
Classification}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Sin-Ze Yang}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
This paper proposes QuSpike-PQK, a projected quantum kernel (PQK) architecture for classifying SARS-CoV-2 spike amino-acid sequences into Pango lineages, and examines whether its quantum component contributes beyond matched classical alternatives. Sequences are encoded as binary mutation indicators relative to Wuhan-Hu-1 and scored by a linear reference classifier that also routes each sequence to a lineage group. For two groups of closely related lineages, a group-wise discriminant projection feeds a parameter-free, Trotterized Heisenberg-type Hamiltonian evolution; single-qubit reduced density matrices define a projected quantum kernel, and kernel support vector machine experts add residual corrections to the reference logits. The design makes the reference a structural lower bound and pairs every quantum component with a classical counterpart. On 31,689 unique sequences from 22 lineages (chronological training/test partition of 22,175/9,514 sequences), QuSpike-PQK reaches a test macro-F1 of \textbf{0.7547}, an accuracy of \textbf{0.6893}, and a ROC AUC of \textbf{0.9745}. On the same 22-class task it exceeds all classical baselines (best: one-hot logistic regression, macro-F1 0.7339) and all image-CNN baselines (best: four-layer CNN with RP, macro-F1 0.6787), by $+0.021$ and $+0.076$ macro-F1, respectively, while training in 3.09 minutes, about 1/29 of the best CNN. This lead, however, comes mainly from the mutation-indicator representation and the linear reference (macro-F1 0.7481); the quantum component contributes only $+0.0066$, and all pre-registered comparisons lie within the 0.02 noise floor: removing entanglement changes macro-F1 by 0.0003, and the classical random-Fourier-feature surrogate scores 0.7552. The quantum feature map is therefore classically replaceable on this task, and we claim neither quantum advantage nor speedup: the circuit uses at most 4 qubits and is simulated exactly (quantum features take 0.37~s, kernel SVMs 185--576~s), so the classical kernel machine remains the bottleneck. In theory, the potential of PQK lies in its resource advantage over other quantum schemes: kernel estimation needs $O(N)$ rather than $O(N^2)$ circuits (for group D, from about $4.5\times10^7$ circuits to the order of $3N$ measurement settings), there are no trainable circuit parameters and hence no barren plateau, and the kernel is exactly invariant to terminal depolarizing noise. We also derive the conditions for a resource crossover relative to classical simulation ($n\gtrsim\log_2 3S$ qubits, a map that cannot be dequantized, and non-concentrated kernel values); none is met on this task. All results come from a single seed and should be read as indicative.
\end{abstract}

Recent work has demonstrated that spike sequences alone can support lineage classification with an alignment-free pipeline based on spiking neural networks (SNNs)~\cite{murad2024snn}. This result shows that spike sequences carry enough information to separate lineages, and it motivates asking whether other model families can add anything on this task.

Biological sequences pose several challenges for machine-learning models. Protein sequences can have different lengths, while many conventional classifiers require fixed-size numerical inputs. Existing pipelines therefore encode or transform sequences before classification. Alignment-based feature engineering can be computationally expensive and dependent on domain-specific choices; signal- and image-based approaches introduce additional transformations. Murad \textit{et al.} address variable-length spike sequences with one-hot encoding and zero padding before applying an SNN classifier~\cite{murad2024snn}. SNNs represent information with temporally precise spikes and have been proposed as biologically inspired alternatives to conventional neural networks~\cite{maass1997,pfeiffer2018}. On such sequence-classification tasks, however, strong classical baselines are already competitive, and whether a quantum machine-learning model adds anything beyond them still lacks a rigorous comparison.

Variational quantum algorithms provide a family of parameterized quantum models that can be integrated into learning systems, but their practical value depends on the task and implementation choices~\cite{cerezo2021vqa}, and their training faces optimization difficulties that make performance differences hard to attribute to the quantum features themselves. The projected quantum kernel (PQK)~\cite{huang2021power} instead uses a fixed circuit with no trainable parameters as a feature map, reads out only single-qubit reduced density matrices, and hands them to a classical kernel machine, so the role of the quantum features can be tested without interference from circuit optimization.

We therefore propose QuSpike-PQK, a projected-quantum-kernel expert architecture that classifies SARS-CoV-2 spike amino-acid sequences into Pango lineages. Sequences are first encoded as mutation-indicator vectors relative to Wuhan-Hu-1, scored by a linear reference classifier, and routed to a lineage group. For the two groups of closely related lineages, a group-wise discriminant projection and a Trotterized Heisenberg-type Hamiltonian evolution build a projected quantum kernel, and kernel support vector machine experts add a residual correction to the reference logits. This work focuses on the following objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Design a PQK residual-expert architecture whose structural lower bound is a strong classical classifier and whose quantum path has no trainable parameters.
    \item Pair every quantum component with a classical counterpart of identical interface (random-Fourier-feature surrogate, entanglement-free circuit, etc.) and pre-register the decision criteria and the stopping rule.
    \item Evaluate on spike sequences from 22 Pango lineages whether the quantum feature map and entanglement contribute beyond classical alternatives, and analyze the computational cost and limitations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

Prior work falls into three threads. For sequence classification, Murad \textit{et al.}~\cite{murad2024snn} study alignment-free spike-sequence classification with an SNN, while Maass~\cite{maass1997} and Pfeiffer and Pfeil~\cite{pfeiffer2018} describe the computational principles of spiking neural networks. For quantum kernel methods, Havl\'{i}\v{c}ek \textit{et al.}~\cite{havlicek2019} build a fidelity kernel from a quantum feature space; Huang \textit{et al.}~\cite{huang2021power} propose the projected quantum kernel, which reads out only local reduced density matrices to mitigate the value concentration of fidelity kernels, and Thanasilp \textit{et al.}~\cite{thanasilp2024concentration} analyze the exponential concentration of quantum kernels. Variational quantum algorithms are reviewed by Cerezo \textit{et al.}~\cite{cerezo2021vqa}, whose trainable circuits face barren plateaus~\cite{mcclean2018barren}. On the replaceability and evaluation of quantum models, Schuld \textit{et al.}~\cite{schuld2021effect} show that data encoding determines a model's spectrum, Schreiber \textit{et al.}~\cite{schreiber2023surrogates} and Sweke \textit{et al.}~\cite{sweke2023rff} show that band-limited quantum models can be approximated by classical surrogates such as random Fourier features, and Bowles \textit{et al.}~\cite{bowles2024better} stress that quantum machine learning must be compared fairly against strong classical baselines. Our design builds on these studies.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
% Method transcribed from Doc/QuSpike-PQK-方法論.md and verified against
% Research/QuSpike_PQK/{features,router,pqk,kernels,experts,pipeline,criteria}.py.

QuSpike-PQK is a \emph{classical--quantum hybrid} architecture: mutation encoding, the linear reference, routing, LDA, and the kernel-SVM experts all run on a classical computer, and only the Hamiltonian evolution that maps the reduced inputs to quantum features runs on a quantum circuit (here, a simulator). In one sentence: \emph{a strong, simple classical classifier answers first, and a quantum-kernel expert corrects it only on the most confusable lineages.} The quantum circuit is a \emph{fixed, parameter-free} feature map inside a residual, group-wise expert architecture. The quantum component is a projected quantum kernel (PQK)~\cite{huang2021power}: a data-dependent Hamiltonian evolution is applied to a small register, single-qubit reduced density matrices are read out, and a classical kernel machine is trained on the readout. Four design principles fix the shape of the architecture:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[P1)] \textit{No trainable quantum parameters.} The circuit depends only on the input and on the hyperparameters $(n,T,\tau,\gamma)$, which removes circuit optimization as an explanation for any outcome.
    \item[P2)] \textit{The strong classical baseline is a structural lower bound.} The quantum path enters as an additive correction whose weight can be zero.
    \item[P3)] \textit{Every quantum component has a classical counterpart} with an identical interface, so that its contribution can be isolated.
    \item[P4)] \textit{Decision criteria and a stopping rule are fixed before evaluation.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Notation}
\label{sec:formulation}

Let $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}=\{(s_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be the training set of spike sequences $s_i$ with lineage labels $y_i\in\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\dots,C\}$, $C=22$, and let $M$ denote the number of test sequences. The label set is partitioned into three groups by a fixed map $\pi:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{G}=\{\mathrm{D},\mathrm{B},\mathrm{O}\}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{D}}$ (Delta): B.1.617.2, AY.103, AY.2, AY.25, AY.3, AY.4, and AY.44 ($C_{\mathrm{D}}=7$);
    \item $\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{B}}$ (B.1 family): B.1, B.1.1, B.1.2, and B.1.177 ($C_{\mathrm{B}}=4$);
    \item $\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{O}}$: the remaining 11 lineages.
\end{itemize}
The two groups $\mathcal{Q}=\{\mathrm{D},\mathrm{B}\}$ consist of closely related lineages on which a linear reference classifier concentrated its errors in an earlier stage of this study; only these groups receive a quantum expert. In Fig.~\ref{fig:lda-scatter}, the projections of AY.103, AY.3, and AY.44 (group D) and of B.1, B.1.1, and B.1.2 (group B) overlap heavily. Group $g\in\mathcal{Q}$ uses $n_g$ qubits, with $n_{\mathrm{D}}=4$ and $n_{\mathrm{B}}=2$; $N_g$ denotes the number of training sequences whose label lies in $\mathcal{Y}_g$.

\subsection{Overall Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:pqk-architecture} summarizes the model. A sequence is encoded as a binary mutation vector $x$, scored by a linear reference classifier, and routed by the reference prediction. Sequences routed to $\mathcal{Q}$ are projected to a low-dimensional discriminant space, mapped through the quantum feature map, and scored by a group-specific kernel expert whose output is added to the reference logits:
\begin{equation}
    \ell(x)=\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}(x)
    +s\sum_{g\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{1}[r(x)=g]\,P_g\,f_g\!\big(\phi_g(z_g(x))\big).
    \label{eq:master}
\end{equation}
Here $\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the reference logit vector (Section~\ref{sec:router}), $r$ the router, $z_g$ the group projection (Section~\ref{sec:projection}), $\phi_g$ the quantum feature map (Section~\ref{sec:encoding}), $f_g$ the kernel expert (Section~\ref{sec:kernel}), $P_g\in\{0,1\}^{C\times C_g}$ the embedding of group scores into the $C$ logits, and $s\ge 0$ a mixing weight selected on validation data (Section~\ref{sec:fusion}).

\subsection{Mutation-Indicator Representation}
\label{sec:mutations}

Mutation encoding has two steps: each sequence is aligned to the reference and its mutations are listed, and the mutations are then encoded as a fixed-length 0/1 vector.

\textbf{Step 1: sequence $\to$ mutation set.} The reference is the Wuhan-Hu-1 spike protein $R$ (NCBI YP\_009724390.1, $|R|=1{,}273$ residues). Sequences of the same length as $R$ are compared position by position; sequences of different length are aligned with Python's \texttt{difflib.SequenceMatcher}. Each difference is recorded as a token $(p,a)$ with 1-based reference position $p$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Substitutions} are recorded as (position, new amino acid), e.g., D614G as (614, G);
    \item \emph{internal deletions} as (position, ``-''), e.g., the 156--157 deletion of Delta;
    \item \emph{terminal truncations} are treated as unobserved, not as deletions; \emph{insertions} are ignored;
    \item positions with the unknown symbol \texttt{X} are discarded.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Step 2: mutation set $\to$ 0/1 vector.} The vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ is built from training sequences only: all distinct (position, amino acid) tokens, sorted, giving $V=|\mathcal{V}|=3{,}686$. For each sequence, the entries of the mutations it carries are set to 1 and all others to 0,
\begin{equation}
    x_v(s)=\mathbb{1}[v\in m(s)],\qquad v\in\mathcal{V},
    \label{eq:mutation}
\end{equation}
where $m(s)$ is the mutation set from Step 1. Test-set mutations absent from the training vocabulary are dropped rather than added as dimensions, so that no test information leaks into the features. For example, a Delta sequence with mutations at L452R, T478K, and D614G has ones at those entries and zeros elsewhere. The 22,175 training sequences form a $22{,}175\times3{,}686$ matrix, which is the input of both the linear reference and the LDA. We also evaluated frozen protein language-model embeddings (ESM-2, 150M parameters, mean-pooled), to be adopted only if they exceeded the mutation-indicator reference by more than 0.02 in macro-F1; they scored lower, so $x$ is used throughout, and all controls share it.

\subsection{Linear Reference and Classical Router}
\label{sec:router}

The reference classifier is a linear softmax model,
\begin{equation}
    \ell_{\mathrm{ref}}(x)=Wx+b,\qquad W\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times V},\ b\in\mathbb{R}^{C},
    \label{eq:reference}
\end{equation}
trained with cross-entropy and AdamW (learning rate $3\times10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch size 512, 60 epochs). It plays three roles: control C0, the structural floor of \eqref{eq:master}, and the router
\begin{equation}
    r(x)=\pi\big(\hat{c}_{\mathrm{ref}}(x)\big),\qquad \hat{c}_{\mathrm{ref}}(x)=\arg\max_{c}\ell_{\mathrm{ref},c}(x).
    \label{eq:router}
\end{equation}
A sequence routed to the wrong group cannot be helped by the correct expert. The routing accuracy
\begin{equation}
    \rho=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathbb{1}\big[r(x_j)=\pi(y_j)\big]
    \label{eq:routing-accuracy}
\end{equation}
therefore bounds the share of test sequences that reach the expert of their true group, and it is reported alongside every result.

\subsection{Group-Wise Discriminant Projection}
\label{sec:projection}

The circuit of group $g$ accepts $2n_g$ real inputs, $n_g$ single-qubit fields and $n_g$ couplings, whereas $V=3{,}686$. For each $g\in\mathcal{Q}$, linear discriminant analysis (LDA)~\cite{fisher1936} is fitted on the training sequences of $\mathcal{Y}_g$ only. Let $A_g\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times k_g}$ collect the leading solutions of $S_{b}a=\lambda S_{w}a$, where $S_b$ and $S_w$ are the between- and within-class scatter matrices, with
\begin{equation}
    k_g=\min(C_g-1,\,2n_g),\qquad k_{\mathrm{D}}=6,\quad k_{\mathrm{B}}=3.
\end{equation}
With $u=A_g^{\top}x$ and per-coordinate training quantiles $q^{\mathrm{lo}}_j$ (1st percentile) and $q^{\mathrm{hi}}_j$ (99th percentile),
\begin{equation}
    z_j=\pi\,\frac{\operatorname{clip}(u_j;\,q^{\mathrm{lo}}_j,q^{\mathrm{hi}}_j)-q^{\mathrm{lo}}_j}{q^{\mathrm{hi}}_j-q^{\mathrm{lo}}_j}-\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad j\le k_g,
    \label{eq:scaling}
\end{equation}
and $z_j=0$ for $k_g<j\le 2n_g$ (zero padding; Fig.~\ref{fig:projection}). The resulting projections of groups D, B, and O are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:lda-scatter}: the ratio of mean centroid distance to within-class standard deviation is as high as 12.9 for group O, far above 2.60 for D and 2.43 for B, consistent with the design in which group O needs no quantum expert and is handled by the classical classifier. LDA is preferred over PCA because, within an already selected group, the directions of largest variance are generally not the discriminative ones. Quantile clipping prevents outliers from compressing the dynamic range, and the interval $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ avoids the endpoint identification of $[-\pi,\pi]$, where rotations by $\pm\pi$ differ only by a global phase.

\subsection{Hamiltonian-Evolution Encoding}
\label{sec:encoding}

For $n=n_g$ qubits and input $z\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]^{2n}$, define the Heisenberg-type Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
    H(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_jZ_j+\sum_{(j,j')\in E}z_{n+j}\,h_{jj'},
    \label{eq:hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    h_{jj'}=X_jX_{j'}+Y_jY_{j'}+Z_jZ_{j'},
\end{equation}
on the ring $E=\{(j,\,j\bmod n+1)\}_{j=1}^{n}$; for $n=2$ the ring has the single edge $(1,2)$. The first $n$ inputs act as local fields and the last $n$ as nearest-neighbor couplings. With $\Delta=\tau/T$, the first-order Trotterized evolution is
\begin{equation}
    U_T(z)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\Bigg[\prod_{(j,j')\in E}e^{-i\Delta z_{n+j}h_{jj'}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}e^{-i\Delta z_jZ_j}\Bigg],
    \label{eq:trotter}
\end{equation}
applied to $|\psi_0\rangle=|{+}\rangle^{\otimes n}=H_{\mathrm{d}}^{\otimes n}|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$, where $H_{\mathrm{d}}$ is the Hadamard gate. We use $T=4$ and $\tau=0.8$. Every factor is native to gate-model hardware: $e^{-i\Delta z Z}=R_Z(2\Delta z)$, and because $XX$, $YY$, and $ZZ$ commute pairwise,
\begin{equation}
    e^{-i\Delta J h_{jj'}}=R_{XX}(2\Delta J)\,R_{YY}(2\Delta J)\,R_{ZZ}(2\Delta J)
\end{equation}
holds exactly. Moreover, $h_{jj'}=2\,\mathrm{SWAP}_{jj'}-I$, so each coupling is a partial swap,
\begin{equation}
    e^{-i\alpha h_{jj'}}=e^{i\alpha}\big[\cos(2\alpha)\,I-i\sin(2\alpha)\,\mathrm{SWAP}_{jj'}\big].
    \label{eq:partial-swap}
\end{equation}
Entanglement therefore arises from the interplay between partial swaps and inhomogeneous fields $z_j\neq z_{j'}$.

The classifier is defined by the circuit $U_T$ itself, so Trotter error is not an approximation error of the model; it only measures how closely the feature map follows the continuous dynamics $e^{-i\tau H(z)}$. The first-order bound~\cite{lloyd1996,childs2021trotter}
\begin{equation}
    \big\|U_T(z)-e^{-i\tau H(z)}\big\|\le\frac{\tau^2}{2T}\sum_{a<b}\big\|[H_a,H_b]\big\|=O\!\left(\frac{n\tau^2}{T}\right)
    \label{eq:trotter-error}
\end{equation}
applies because each term $H_a$ has norm $O(1)$ for $|z_j|\le\pi/2$ and fails to commute with only $O(1)$ neighboring terms. Against exact evolution ($n=3$, $\tau=0.7$), the measured maximum error $\max|\phi-\phi_{\mathrm{exact}}|$ (mean over 40 random $z$) decreases from $1.93\times10^{-1}$ at $T=4$ to $3.05\times10^{-3}$ at $T=256$ (log-log slope $-0.997$), consistent with $O(1/T)$.

\textit{Projected readout.} With $|\psi(z)\rangle=U_T(z)|\psi_0\rangle$ and $\rho_k(z)=\operatorname{Tr}_{\neg k}|\psi(z)\rangle\langle\psi(z)|$ the one-qubit reduced density matrix (1-RDM), $\rho_k=\tfrac12(I+\phi_k\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})$ with Bloch vector
\begin{equation}
    \phi_k(z)=\big(\langle X_k\rangle,\langle Y_k\rangle,\langle Z_k\rangle\big)_{\psi(z)},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \phi(z)=\big(\phi_1(z),\dots,\phi_n(z)\big)\in[-1,1]^{3n}.
    \label{eq:readout}
\end{equation}
The feature vector is thus exactly the collection of 1-RDMs. Two structural properties of this map (Fig.~\ref{fig:viz-encoding} shows the circuit and numerical examples) follow directly from \eqref{eq:hamiltonian}--\eqref{eq:trotter}.

\textit{Proposition 1 (conserved magnetization).} For all $z$,
\[
    \sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle Z_k\rangle_{\psi(z)}=0.
\]

\textit{Proof.} The total magnetization $\sum_k Z_k$ commutes with every $Z_j$ and, by the SU(2) invariance of $h_{jj'}$, with every coupling gate; hence it is conserved by $U_T(z)$. Its expectation in $|{+}\rangle^{\otimes n}$ is zero. $\blacksquare$

Consequently, $\phi(z)$ lies in a hyperplane of dimension $3n-1$; for $n=2$, $\langle Z_1\rangle=-\langle Z_2\rangle$.

\textit{Proposition 2 (product-state limit).} If all couplings vanish, then for every $T$
\begin{equation}
    \phi_k(z)=\big(\cos 2\tau z_k,\ \sin 2\tau z_k,\ 0\big).
    \label{eq:product-limit}
\end{equation}

\textit{Proof.} The qubits evolve independently under $R_Z(2\tau z_k)$, whose action on $|{+}\rangle$ rotates the Bloch vector $(1,0,0)$ by angle $2\tau z_k$ about the $z$-axis. $\blacksquare$

The implementation agrees with \eqref{eq:product-limit} to $2.22\times10^{-15}$. Because $2\tau|z_k|\le0.8\pi<\pi$, this map is injective on $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$. Proposition~2 also fixes a consequence of zero padding: in group D, $k_{\mathrm{D}}=6<8$ gives $z_7=z_8=0$, so the couplings on edges $(3,4)$ and $(4,1)$ vanish. Qubit 4 is then decoupled, its readout is given by \eqref{eq:product-limit}, and entangling dynamics are confined to the path $1$--$2$--$3$. In group B, $k_{\mathrm{B}}=3$ fills the two fields and the single coupling, so padding does not remove an edge.

\textit{Frequency content.} Following Schuld \textit{et al.}~\cite{schuld2021effect}, each entry of $\phi$ is a trigonometric polynomial of $z$. A field input enters through $T$ generators $\Delta Z$ with eigenvalue gap $2\Delta$, and a coupling input through $T$ generators $\Delta h$ whose eigenvalues $\{1,-3\}$ have gap $4\Delta$. The accessible frequencies are therefore $\{2\Delta m:|m|\le T\}$ (maximum $2\tau$) for fields and $\{4\Delta m:|m|\le T\}$ (maximum $4\tau$) for couplings. This band limit is what admits classical surrogates (Section~\ref{sec:controls}).

\subsection{Projected Quantum Kernel and Group Experts}
\label{sec:kernel}

Huang \textit{et al.}~\cite{huang2021power} define the projected quantum kernel
\begin{equation}
    k^{\mathrm{PQ}}(z,z')=\exp\!\Big(-\gamma'\sum_{k=1}^{n}\big\|\rho_k(z)-\rho_k(z')\big\|_F^2\Big).
\end{equation}
Since $\|\rho_k-\rho_k'\|_F^2=\tfrac12\|\phi_k-\phi_k'\|_2^2$, this is the Gaussian kernel on the readout,
\begin{equation}
    \kappa(z,z')=\exp\!\big(-\gamma\,\|\phi(z)-\phi(z')\|_2^2\big),\qquad\gamma=\gamma'/2,
    \label{eq:pqk}
\end{equation}
which is positive definite by construction. The bandwidth follows the median heuristic,
\begin{equation}
    \gamma=\Big(2\operatorname*{median}_{a<b}\|\phi_a-\phi_b\|_2^2\Big)^{-1},
    \label{eq:median-gamma}
\end{equation}
estimated on 512 training samples of the group. Projection to local 1-RDMs, rather than the fidelity kernel $|\langle\psi(z)|\psi(z')\rangle|^2$~\cite{havlicek2019}, is the mechanism proposed in~\cite{huang2021power} to mitigate the kernel-value concentration of large Hilbert spaces.

For each $g\in\mathcal{Q}$, the expert $f_g$ is a multiclass support vector machine with precomputed Gram matrix $K_g=[\kappa(z_a,z_b)]\in\mathbb{R}^{N_g\times N_g}$, regularization $C_{\mathrm{SVM}}=10$, pairwise training, and one-vs-rest aggregated decision scores
\begin{equation}
    f_{g,c}(x)=\sum_{a\in\mathrm{SV}}\beta_{c,a}\,\kappa\big(z_g(x),z_a\big)+b_c,\qquad c\in\mathcal{Y}_g.
    \label{eq:svm}
\end{equation}
Floating-point round-off can break positive semidefiniteness; if $\lambda_{\min}(K_g)<-10^{-8}$, negative eigenvalues are clipped and the diagonal is renormalized to one, and the correction is logged rather than applied silently. The mean and standard deviation of off-diagonal entries are recorded as a concentration diagnostic. When $8N_g^2$ bytes exceed a 2-GB budget, a Nystr\"om approximation~\cite{williams2001nystrom} with 2,048 landmarks is used; the largest group (D, $N_{\mathrm{D}}=9{,}538$) fits exactly (364~MB in single precision).

\subsection{Residual Logit Fusion and Inference}
\label{sec:fusion}

Inference follows \eqref{eq:master}: the expert scores of the routed group are written into that group's $C_g$ logit columns, scaled by $s$, and added to the reference logits; the prediction is $\hat{y}(x)=\arg\max_c\ell_c(x)$ with class probabilities $\operatorname{softmax}(\ell(x))$. SVM decision values and linear logits have unrelated scales, so $s$ must be selected rather than fixed. It is chosen from
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{S}=\{0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,1,2,4\}
\end{equation}
by macro-F1 on a validation split consisting of the latest 15\% of each lineage's training sequences by collection date. For this search, the reference, projection, and experts are refitted on the remaining 85\%; because the expert fit does not depend on $s$, it is fitted once and rescaled across $\mathcal{S}$. The selected $s$ is then used with components refitted on the full training set. All configurations share the same grid, split, and selection rule. Algorithm~1 summarizes the procedure.

\textit{Proposition 3 (structural floor and locality).} (i) For $s=0$, \eqref{eq:master} reduces to $\ell_{\mathrm{ref}}$; since $0\in\mathcal{S}$, the selected configuration's validation macro-F1 is at least that of the reference. (ii) If $r(x)=\mathrm{O}$, then $\hat{y}(x)=\hat{c}_{\mathrm{ref}}(x)$. (iii) If $r(x)=g\in\mathcal{Q}$, only the $C_g$ logits of group $g$ are modified.

Part (i) holds on validation data, not as a test-set guarantee. It separates a lack of quantum benefit from optimization failure: a model that cannot exceed the reference has not been prevented from doing so by its architecture. Part (ii) is checked empirically: predictions for sequences routed to O are identical to C0 for every configuration.

\subsection{Controls and Classical Surrogates}
\label{sec:controls}

All controls share the representation $x$, router, projection, classifier type, and mixing grid; they differ only in the feature map applied to $z_g$ (Table~\ref{tab:pqk-controls}).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{C1} applies the Gaussian kernel directly to $z_g$, testing whether a feature map helps at all.
    \item \textit{C2} is a random-Fourier-feature (RFF) surrogate~\cite{rahimi2007rff,schreiber2023surrogates,sweke2023rff},
    \begin{equation}
        \phi^{\mathrm{RFF}}(z)=\big[\cos(\Omega^{\top}z+\beta),\ \sin(\Omega^{\top}z+\beta)\big]_{1:3n},
    \end{equation}
    with $\Omega_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(2\tau)^2)$ and $\beta_i\sim\mathcal{U}[0,2\pi)$. Its scale matches the field band limit $2\tau$, and its output dimension and range match $\phi$. Because band-limited quantum models admit such surrogates~\cite{schreiber2023surrogates}, parity with C2 means that the quantum map is classically replaceable on this task.
    \item \textit{C3} removes all couplings. By Proposition~2, its kernel has the closed form
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa_{\mathrm{C3}}(z,z')=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\exp\!\big(-4\gamma\sin^2\!\big(\tau(z_k-z_k')\big)\big),
        \label{eq:c3-kernel}
    \end{equation}
    a classical, stationary, periodic kernel. MAIN$-$C3 is therefore the only comparison that can attribute a gain to entanglement. C3 also discards the $n$ coupling inputs, so a positive gap would not isolate entanglement alone, whereas a null gap is the stronger finding: the entangled map gains nothing despite seeing more inputs.
    \item \textit{C4} is a width-matched MLP (one $\tanh$ hidden layer of width $3n_g$) on $z_g$, with centered log-probabilities as scores.
    \item \textit{C5} uses a single PQK expert for all 22 lineages ($n=4$, $k=8$) without grouping.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pre-Registered Decision Criteria}
\label{sec:criteria}

For configurations $A$ and $B$ with mean test macro-F1 $F_A$, $F_B$ and seed standard deviations $\sigma_A$, $\sigma_B$, let $\Delta_{AB}=F_A-F_B$ and $\delta=0.02$, a noise floor estimated from repeated GPU runs in earlier experiments. A claim \emph{holds} if $\Delta_{AB}>\delta$ and $\Delta_{AB}>\max(\sigma_A,\sigma_B)$, is \emph{within noise} if $|\Delta_{AB}|\le\max(\delta,\sigma_A,\sigma_B)$, and \emph{fails} otherwise. The four claims are: the framework is useful (MAIN vs.\ C0); the quantum features are useful (MAIN vs.\ $\max\{\mathrm{C1},\mathrm{C2},\mathrm{C4}\}$, the strongest classical alternative); entanglement is useful (MAIN vs.\ C3); and grouping is useful (MAIN vs.\ C5). If neither quantum claim holds, PQK is declared classically replaceable on this task, and enlarging $n$ or retuning $(T,\tau,\gamma)$ to reverse the outcome is not permitted.

\subsection{Quantum Advantages and Limitations}
\label{sec:quantum-tradeoffs}

\textit{Shot noise.} Each entry of $\phi$ is the mean of $S$ outcomes in $\{\pm1\}$. By Hoeffding's inequality and a union bound over $3n$ entries, with probability at least $1-\eta$,
\begin{equation}
    \|\hat{\phi}-\phi\|_\infty\le\varepsilon_S=\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(6n/\eta)}{S}}.
\end{equation}
Since $\|\phi\|_2\le\sqrt{n}$ and $t\mapsto e^{-\gamma t}$ is $\gamma$-Lipschitz on $t\ge0$, the kernel error between two samples satisfies
\begin{equation}
    |\hat{\kappa}-\kappa|\le\gamma\,e\,(4\sqrt{n}+e),\qquad e\le2\sqrt{3n}\,\varepsilon_S,
\end{equation}
that is, $|\hat{\kappa}-\kappa|=O(\gamma\,n\,\varepsilon_S)$. Under the median heuristic, $\gamma\propto1/\operatorname{median}\|\phi_a-\phi_b\|^2$, which is $\Theta(1/n)$ when readouts are not concentrated. The kernel error is then $O(\sqrt{\log(n/\eta)/S})$, and $S=O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(n/\eta))$ shots per setting suffice for kernel accuracy $\varepsilon$.

\textit{Proposition 4 (invariance to terminal depolarizing noise).} Let the local depolarizing channel $\mathcal{N}_p(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+\tfrac{p}{3}(X\rho X+Y\rho Y+Z\rho Z)$ act on every qubit after $U_T$. If $\gamma$ is re-estimated by \eqref{eq:median-gamma} on the noisy readout, the Gram matrix is unchanged.

\textit{Proof.} $\mathcal{N}_p$ maps each Bloch vector $\phi_k$ to $c\,\phi_k$ with $c=1-4p/3$. Hence all squared distances scale by $c^2$, the median-heuristic $\gamma$ by $c^{-2}$, and $\gamma\|\phi_a-\phi_b\|^2$ is unchanged. $\blacksquare$

Numerically, Gram matrices are bitwise identical for $p\le0.1$. The invariance does not extend to noise interleaved with entangling gates, amplitude damping, coherent over-rotations, or asymmetric readout error, which do not act as a uniform rescaling; it also makes a sweep over $p$ uninformative for this pipeline.

\textit{Proposition 5 (resource counting and crossover).} Let $N$ be the number of training samples, $M$ the number of test samples, and $S$ the number of shots per setting. (i) Each entry $|\langle\psi(z)|\psi(z')\rangle|^2$ of the fidelity kernel~\cite{havlicek2019} needs one compute--uncompute circuit $U_T^{\dagger}(z')U_T(z)$, so the Gram matrix needs $N(N-1)/2$ circuits and $S\,N(N-1)/2=\Theta(SN^2)$ shots in total; for group D ($N=9{,}538$) this is $4.55\times10^7$ circuits. (ii) PQK needs only the three settings $X^{\otimes n}$, $Y^{\otimes n}$, and $Z^{\otimes n}$ per sample (each setting yields all $n$ Pauli expectations of one type), for $3S(N+M)=\Theta(SN)$ shots in total; the ratio of the two is $\frac{N(N-1)}{6(N+M)}=\Theta(N)$. (iii) If hardware costs $3S\cdot O(Tn)$ gate operations per sequence and state-vector simulation costs $O(Tn\,2^{n})$, a necessary condition for hardware to beat exact simulation in resources is $2^{n}\gtrsim 3S$, i.e., $n\gtrsim\log_2(3S)$; for $S=10^{3}$--$10^{4}$ this means $n\gtrsim12$--$15$, far above $n\le4$ here, where $2^n=16\ll3S$.

Parts (i) and (ii) follow by direct counting; (iii) is the ratio of the two cost expressions, and the resulting $n$ is only the crossover against exact state-vector simulation. It does not by itself amount to quantum advantage, which would additionally require: (a) a map that cannot be dequantized, i.e., lies outside the band-limited regime that RFF can approximate~\cite{schreiber2023surrogates,sweke2023rff}, and whose single-qubit marginals cannot be computed in polynomial time from shallow light cones (L1); (b) kernel values that do not concentrate exponentially in $n$~\cite{thanasilp2024concentration}, since otherwise the required $S$ grows with $1/\gamma$ by the shot-noise bound above; (c) discriminative information that genuinely resides in the quantum features, i.e., outperforming classical surrogates, which, as shown by Huang \textit{et al.}~\cite{huang2021power}, requires data-dependent quantum structure. The C2 and C3 controls show that, for $n\le4$ on this dataset, (a) and (c) are not met, so (i)--(iii) should be read as a resource potential rather than a measured advantage.

\textit{Advantages.} (A1) Without trainable circuit parameters, there are no barren plateaus~\cite{mcclean2018barren} or gradient-estimation costs, and features are deterministic, cacheable, and reproducible. (A2) Local projection requires $O(N)$ rather than $O(N^2)$ quantum circuits (Proposition~5) and mitigates the concentration of fidelity kernels~\cite{huang2021power}. (A3) Under the median heuristic, the kernel is robust to shot noise, with $O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log n)$ shots, and exactly invariant to terminal local depolarizing noise (Proposition~4). (A4) The circuit uses native gates ($R_Z$, $R_{XX}$, $R_{YY}$, $R_{ZZ}$) on nearest-neighbor ring connectivity with $O(Tn)$ gates. (A5) With couplings, the 1-RDMs depend on correlations generated by entangling dynamics that are unavailable to product-state encodings; this is the only point at which the design permits quantumness to matter, and C3 tests it directly.

\textit{Limitations.} (L1) \emph{Classical simulability.} At $n\le4$, the model is simulated exactly at negligible cost. Scaling $n$ at fixed $T$ does not by itself make it hard to simulate: single-qubit marginals of shallow nearest-neighbor circuits can be computed from their backward light cones in time exponential only in the depth. (L2) \emph{Dequantization.} The band-limited spectrum of Section~\ref{sec:encoding} admits RFF surrogates~\cite{schreiber2023surrogates,sweke2023rff}, which C2 instantiates. (L3) \emph{Information bottleneck.} The circuit sees only $k_g\le2n_g$ LDA coordinates of a $V=3{,}686$-dimensional input. By the data-processing inequality, $I(y;\phi(z_g))\le I(y;z_g)\le I(y;x)$, so the quantum map cannot recover discriminative information discarded by the projection. Proposition~1 further reduces the readout to $3n-1$ effective dimensions, and zero padding decouples one qubit in group D. (L4) \emph{Concentration at scale.} For larger, more entangling evolutions, 1-RDMs approach the maximally mixed state, $\phi\to0$, and kernel values concentrate~\cite{thanasilp2024concentration}; the median-heuristic $\gamma$ then grows and amplifies the shot-noise bound above. (L5) \emph{Hardware cost.} Each sequence needs three settings of $S$ shots. A dry-run estimate for the Delta test set alone is about 3.5~h of device time in 164 jobs, and the classical $O(N_g^2)$ kernel machine remains. (L6) \emph{Positioning.} The framework claims no quantum advantage or speedup. Its purpose is to make parity or gain decidable on a real task with strong classical baselines, in line with recent benchmarking guidance~\cite{bowles2024better}.

\subsection{Filtering and Deduplication}

Sequences were retained if they contained 1,200--1,300 residues, used only the 20 standard amino-acid symbols or \texttt{X}, and had at most 5\% unknown residues. Exact duplicates were collapsed while retaining the earliest collection date, and sequences with conflicting lineage labels were removed. Each lineage was randomly capped at 3,000 unique sequences using seed 0; lineages with fewer than 30 sequences were excluded. All reported counts refer to unique sequences.

\subsection{Data Partition and Evaluation Metrics}

Within each lineage, sequences were ordered by collection date and split chronologically into approximately 70\% training and 30\% testing, yielding 22,175 and 9,514 sequences, respectively. Undated sequences were assigned to training. The dataset documentation reports no exact sequence overlap between partitions. This split uses lineage-specific temporal boundaries; partial dates and closely related sequences limit temporal independence.

Macro-F1 is the intended primary metric, with accuracy as a complementary measure. Validation, hyperparameter selection, and representation pretraining must use training data only. Variable sequence lengths and unknown residues require consistent handling to reduce confounding. Because the data source, sampling, and partition protocol differ from Spike7k~\cite{murad2024snn}, performance values are not directly comparable with its published results.

\subsection{Baseline Models}
\label{sec:baselines}

The reported experiments were run on a system equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5060 Laptop GPU with 8~GB of VRAM.

To assess QuSpike-PQK, we consider two baseline families that use different representations of spike sequences:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Embedding-based classification.} Classical classifiers use either one-hot sequence representations or 128-dimensional autoencoder embeddings. The autoencoder learns to reconstruct its input through a dense encoder with widths 1024--512--256--128, trained for 100 epochs. Each representation is classified directly.
    \item \textit{Image-based classification.} Sequences are converted into cumulative-sum signals and then into recurrence plots (RP), Gramian angular fields (GAF), or Markov transition fields (MTF). The resulting images are resized to $360\times360$ pixels with three channels and classified by a three-layer CNN, a four-layer CNN, or an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet34 ($3\times3$ configurations in total). Figure~\ref{fig:image-transformation} illustrates the three image representations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Result and Discussion}
\label{sec:result}
% Baseline results are transcribed from references/classical_overview.md and
% references/cnn_overview.md. QuSpike-PQK results are from
% Results/quspike_pqk/pqk_{MAIN,C0..C5}__mut__seed0.json.

Table~\ref{tab:classification-results} compares the classical, image-CNN, and QuSpike-PQK results on the same 22-class task. The classical and CNN baselines use 22,154 training and 9,503 test sequences (common length 1,266 after excluding short sequences), which differ slightly from the 22,175/9,514 of Section~\ref{sec:dataset}; the task and lineages are the same, so the results are directly comparable.

\textbf{Embedding-based baselines.} One-hot LR achieves the highest macro-F1 (0.7339), while RF performs best on autoencoder embeddings (0.5696); all seven classifiers obtain higher macro-F1 with one-hot inputs than with autoencoder embeddings. However, one-hot LR reached the iteration limit without convergence, and MLP also produced convergence warnings.

\textbf{Image-based baselines.} All nine CNN configurations use seed 0 only. The best is the four-layer CNN with RP (macro-F1 0.6787, accuracy 0.6333), almost level with GAF (0.6755); MTF is clearly worse (0.465--0.569). Deeper networks help (the four-layer CNN is about 0.04--0.08 above the three-layer one), but ResNet34 is not better (0.6633 with RP), and its training loss is far below that of the four-layer CNN, indicating overfitting. The best image-CNN macro-F1 is below the best one-hot classical method (0.7339), yet its training time is one to two orders of magnitude higher (80--156 minutes). Ancestral and Delta sublineages such as AY.103, B.1, B.1.1, B.1.617.2, AY.3, and AY.44 are the hardest to separate for every CNN.

\textbf{QuSpike-PQK ranks first on all metrics.} On the same 22-class task, QuSpike-PQK reaches a macro-F1 of \textbf{0.7547}, which is $+0.021$ above the best classical baseline (one-hot LR, 0.7339) and $+0.076$ above the best image CNN (four-layer CNN with RP, 0.6787). Its accuracy is \textbf{0.6893} (best classical 0.6783, best CNN 0.6333), macro precision \textbf{0.7692}, macro recall \textbf{0.7594}, and ROC AUC \textbf{0.9745}, all the highest in the table (Fig.~\ref{fig:compare}). Training takes only \textbf{3.09 minutes}, about 29 times faster than the best CNN, about 49 times faster than ResNet34, and below the 6.17 minutes of one-hot LR.

QuSpike-PQK uses the full partition of Section~\ref{sec:dataset} (22,175 training and 9,514 test sequences, 22 lineages) and seed 0 only. It reaches a macro-F1 of 0.7547, an accuracy of 0.6893, and a ROC AUC of 0.9745, with a validation-selected mixing weight $s=0.25$. Its routing accuracy is 0.9667. Relative to its own linear reference (C0, macro-F1 0.7481), the gain of +0.0066 comes from the two quantum groups: group macro-F1 rises from 0.601 to 0.612 for Delta and from 0.550 to 0.567 for the B.1 family, while the remaining 11 lineages stay at 0.949 with predictions identical to C0. The matched classical controls perform equally well: the RFF surrogate C2 scores 0.7552, the entanglement-free circuit C3 0.7544, the Gaussian kernel C1 0.7534, the width-matched MLP C4 0.7489, and the ungrouped PQK C5 0.7481. All four pre-registered claims of Section~\ref{sec:criteria} are therefore within noise (framework $+0.0066$, quantum features $-0.0005$, entanglement $+0.0003$, grouping $+0.0066$), which triggers the stopping rule: on this task, the projected quantum kernel is classically replaceable. The quantum features take 0.37~s of the 185~s total runtime; the remainder is spent in the classical reference and kernel SVMs. These results come from a single seed and should be read as indicative (Fig.~\ref{fig:results-viz}). Figure~\ref{fig:gram} compares the Gram matrices of the three groups: in all three, the gap between mean same-lineage and cross-lineage kernel values of MAIN (D 0.222, B 0.176, O 0.725) is no higher than that of the classical RBF (0.244, 0.249, 0.733), so it is no evidence that the quantum kernel has better structure. In group D, AY.25 and AY.4 form their own blocks, but kernel values among AY.103, AY.3, and AY.44 are high (about 0.6--0.8) and the lineages are hard to separate under both kernels; in group B, with only 2 qubits and a 3-dimensional LDA, the cross-lineage kernel value of MAIN is as high as 0.404 and the kernel is blurry, with only B.1.177 clearly separated; in group O, both kernels give nearly perfect block-diagonal matrices, with a clear high-similarity block only between B.1.427 and B.1.429.
